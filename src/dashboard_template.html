Can an already trained AI model help a grocery store forecast sales?

I tested Google's TimesFM 3.0 on four product groups at one real grocery store. I compared it with repeating last week's sales and with a machine-learning model trained on earlier sales.

The result: TimesFM with calendar information and the fitted machine-learning model had almost the same total error. Calendar information helped TimesFM. Adding promotions and past transactions did not improve that version in this test.

SITUATION AND TASK

A store has to order before it knows what will sell.

Too little stock can leave shelves empty. Too much stock can increase holding costs and waste. A useful forecast helps decide what to order.

TimesFM has already learned patterns from other time-series data. The question was whether it could provide useful sales forecasts here without training a new forecasting model on this dataset.

The data comes from Favorita store 1 in Quito, Ecuador. I used beverages, bread/bakery, dairy, and grocery I.

These are product groups, rather than individual products. Recorded sales measure quantity, sometimes including fractions. They do not tell us everything customers would have bought if the store had always had stock.

ACTION

Give each method the past, then check its next two weeks.

TimesFM saw 256 days of sales history and predicted the next 14 days. I repeated the test at twelve weekly starting dates in 2017. Later sales and transaction counts were hidden until they became part of the past.

I also tested what happened when TimesFM considered the product groups together and when it received calendar information. A final version added promotions and past transaction counts.

RESULT: FORECASTS

TimesFM improved on the weekly rule. The fitted model was very close.

The score adds up the size of all forecast mistakes and divides by total recorded sales. TimesFM with calendar information made 22.9% less total error than the weekly rule in this test.

MethodTotal forecast error, lower is better

This score is called WAPE in the technical results. It does not mean every forecast is within that percentage of the answer. Forecasts overlap by one week. The separate first-week check, which avoids repeated target dates, gives 9.10% for calendar-aware TimesFM and 9.18% for gradient boosting.

Where it helped, and where it struggled.

Results varied by product group.

TimesFM with calendar information did better than gradient boosting on beverages and bread/bakery. Gradient boosting did better on dairy and grocery I.

Considering the groups together helped only a little. Adding promotions and past transactions made the calendar version's total error worse. This test changes those two inputs together, so it cannot explain which one mattered.

Some individual days were far off.

For August 11, a forecast made on August 2 predicted about 2,120 beverage units. The recorded quantity was 1,006. That observation also fell below the estimated middle-80% range.

The range is an estimate for each day. Real sales can fall outside it. This project documents the miss but does not establish what caused it.

RESULT: A SIMPLE INVENTORY SIMULATION

Would the forecasts lead to useful stocking decisions?

I simulated weekly orders with a seven-day delivery time. Each policy orders enough for its next fourteen-day sales forecast, plus extra stock estimated from earlier mistakes. Every method starts with the same stock.

TimesFM with the groups together had the lowest cost under the default assumptions: 20.4% below the weekly rule. It fulfilled 99.73% of recorded sales quantity, compared with 98.61% for that rule. This is a simulation using recorded sales as a substitute for demand.

The lowest forecast error and the lowest simulated inventory cost came from different TimesFM versions. The inventory policy uses errors in earlier fourteen-day totals to estimate extra stock. It does not use TimesFM's daily uncertainty bands directly.

What this experiment supports.

TimesFM was a useful forecasting candidate on this store and period, without task-specific training. Its calendar version performed close to a fitted machine-learning model. The extra inputs did not all help.

This covers one store and four product groups. The comparison does not establish which model works best across retail. ARIMA and exponential smoothing were not tested. The public data could overlap Google's earlier training data.

Inventory costs are simulated. The replay leaves out expiry, capacity, buying costs, and the value of remaining stock at the end. Different costs can change which policy looks best. The downloadable TimesFM 3.0 weights also carry a non-commercial, non-production license.
