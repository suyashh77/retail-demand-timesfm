# Short STAR answer

**Situation.** I heard about Google's TimesFM 3.0, which uses a model already trained on other time-series data to predict future values. I wanted to test it on retail sales, where the forecast affects how much stock a store orders.

**Task.** I wanted to find out whether it could compete with a simple weekly forecast and a fitted machine-learning model, whether extra inputs helped, and what its forecasts would mean for inventory.

**Analysis.** I used four product groups from one Favorita grocery store in Ecuador. At twelve weekly starting dates, I predicted the next fourteen days. TimesFM received 256 days of history. I compared separate and joint forecasts, then added calendar information, promotions, and historical transactions. The comparison models repeated the previous week or used gradient boosting fitted only to earlier labeled sales. Future promotions were assumed known because dated plans were unavailable. I also replayed weekly stock orders with a seven-day delivery time and tested the input boundaries and inventory balances.

**Result.** Calendar-aware TimesFM had 9.68% total forecast error, compared with 12.56% for the weekly rule and 9.69% for gradient boosting. That was a useful result without task-specific TimesFM training, but it did not establish an advantage over the fitted model. TimesFM did better on beverages and bread/bakery, while gradient boosting did better on dairy and grocery I. Adding the full input bundle did not help. A different TimesFM version had the lowest simulated inventory cost under the default assumptions, 20.4% below the weekly rule. I would describe that as a conditional simulation result, rather than actual business savings. The next step would be other stores, a new test period, and timestamped promotion plans.
